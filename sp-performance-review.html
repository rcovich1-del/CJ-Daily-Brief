Section 2

Start here if these words are new

A sector is a group of companies that do broadly similar things. Every company in the S&P 500 is sorted into exactly one of eleven sectors under a classification system called GICS. Banks and insurers sit in Financials. Chipmakers and software firms sit in Information Technology. Oil producers sit in Energy. Sorting companies this way lets you see which parts of the economy investors rewarded and which they did not.
A sector ETF is a single fund you can buy that holds all the companies in one sector at once. The tickers in this report, such as XLK for technology or XLE for energy, are the best known family of these funds. They are mentioned here only so you can recognise the shorthand when you see it elsewhere.
Price return versus total return. Price return counts only the change in share price. Total return also counts dividends, the cash payments many companies send shareholders, and assumes you used that cash to buy more shares. Total return is always the higher of the two, and the gap is widest in sectors that pay large dividends such as Utilities and Real Estate. Every return in this report is a total return unless a label says otherwise, and mixing the two without saying so is one of the most common ways market figures mislead people.
Forward P/E stands for forward price to earnings ratio. Take what a company costs to buy and divide it by the profit analysts expect it to make over the coming year. A forward P/E of 20 means investors are paying twenty dollars for every one dollar of expected annual profit. A higher number means investors are paying more for each dollar of expected profit, which usually signals they expect fast growth. It does not by itself mean something is good value or poor value.
Why a rising market can get cheaper. The forward P/E is a fraction. Share prices sit on top, expected profits sit underneath. If expected profits grow faster than prices rise, the fraction shrinks even as the market goes up. That is exactly what happened in the first half of 2026, and it is why the S&P 500 gained more than ten percent while its forward P/E fell from 22.0 to 20.1.
Concentration measures how much of a sector sits in just a few companies. When the ten largest holdings are 73 percent of a sector, as they are in Energy, that sector's performance is mostly the story of ten businesses rather than the whole industry.

Section 7

Which sectors re rated and which de rated

Comparing what investors paid for a dollar of expected profit at the start of the year against what they pay now. A bar pointing left means the sector became cheaper on this measure.

Two things worth understanding about this chart

Why only four series. A before and after comparison is only honest when both numbers are measured the same way. FactSet publishes start of year and current forward P/E on a consistent basis, but its weekly report names only the most and least expensive sectors in text, and its full eleven sector chart is published as an image rather than as figures. That leaves verified matched pairs for three sectors and the index. The all eleven forward P/E figures used elsewhere in this report were computed from constituent data on a different estimate horizon, so placing them on this chart would produce a change that is partly a measurement artefact rather than a real move. They have been left off rather than presented as if comparable.

How a sector can rise in price and still get cheaper. The S&P 500 line is the clearest example in the data. The index returned +10.21% over the period, yet its forward P/E fell from 22.0 to 20.1. Prices went up, but the profits analysts expect over the coming year went up faster, so each dollar of expected profit costs less than it did in January. Consensus expectations for S&P 500 earnings growth in 2026 were revised from roughly 15% at the start of the year to 27.3% by late July. Part of that jump reflects a single one off item, a $98 billion gain recorded by Alphabet, which FactSet notes lifted the quarterly figure substantially.

              What the pattern shows

              No sector held a top three place across all three periods. Communication Services led in 2024 and stayed in the top three in 2025, then fell to the bottom three in the current period.               Spotlight: Information Technology

              The largest sector in the index, one of the strongest performers over the period, and the source of the most counter intuitive numbers in this dataset.

              What has been working

              • The sector reported the highest revenue growth of all eleven sectors in the second quarter of 2026 at +34.7% year over year, and blended earnings growth of +64.6%, third highest of the eleven.
              • Semiconductors and semiconductor equipment drove most of it, with earnings up 134% and revenue up 76% year over year. Strip semiconductors out and the sector's earnings growth falls to 26.1%.
              • Net profit margin reached 30.8% against a five year average of 25.6%.
              • All six industries inside the sector grew earnings, from software at +17% to hardware and storage at +45%.

              What commentators say could derail it

              • FactSet reported on 23 Jul 2026 that combined capital spending by the five largest cloud companies is set to exceed $690bn this financial year, and that free cash flow is expected to move close to zero or turn negative for all of them except Alphabet and Microsoft.
              • Debt has moved from 9% of that spending in FY24 to 32% over the year to mid 2026, per the same FactSet analysis.
              • Investor Michael Burry argues those companies write down their chips too slowly, understating costs by roughly $176bn across 2026 to 2028. FactSet notes the market evidence currently points the other way, with second hand chip prices holding up.
              • Axios reported on 27 Jul 2026 that Nvidia is weighing guarantees of $250bn for an OpenAI data centre project, reviving concerns about circular financing between chip sellers and buyers. Nvidia shares fell 4.5% and the cost of insuring its debt posted its largest intraday rise since that market became active.
              • Interest rate futures moved to price meaningful odds of a rate rise rather than a cut, which historically matters more for companies valued on distant future profits.

              The number that surprises people most

              This sector returned nearly twenty percent over the six month period, yet on 28 July the median company inside it was 29.4% below its own 52 week high and exactly half of them were down 30% or more. Both facts are true at once because the sector is weighted by company size. A small number of very large companies carried the return while a great many smaller ones fell a long way. For comparison, across the whole S&P 500 the median company was 11.9% below its high. A second oddity sits alongside it: the sector's trailing P/E of 33.41 falls to a forward P/E of 18.79, which is simply another way of saying analysts expect profits here to grow very sharply. If those profits arrive, today's price looks different than if they do not.

              Section 10

              How the next half could play out

              Three frameworks that named, dated commentators are actually using. These are their published views, reported here with attribution. They are not this report's views and no probabilities are attached to any of them.

              Framework one: the earnings path

              This framing holds that company profits, rather than investor enthusiasm, are doing the work. Ed Yardeni raised his year end 2026 S&P 500 projection to 8,250 on 10 May 2026 and repeated it on 7 July, describing the move as earnings led and saying of artificial intelligence that it is "the real deal." His arithmetic applies a multiple of about 20 to expected 2027 earnings of roughly $400 per share. Ben Snider of Goldman Sachs raised his own figure to 8,000 on 28 May 2026, projecting 2026 earnings of $340 and 2027 earnings of $385, and attributing about half of the earnings expansion to spending on artificial intelligence infrastructure. Snider also noted that excluding that infrastructure and energy, 2027 estimates are flat so far this year.

              Sectors this framing usually involves: Information Technology, Communication Services and Industrials.

              Ed Yardeni, Yardeni Research, 10 May and 7 Jul 2026. Ben Snider, Goldman Sachs, 28 May and 29 Jun 2026.

              Framework two: the interest rate path

              This framing puts the cost of borrowing at the centre. Through July 2026 futures markets moved sharply, and by 27 July were pricing roughly a 64% chance that the Federal Reserve holds rates at 3.50% to 3.75% at its 29 July meeting and a 36% chance of an increase. CNBC reported on 23 July that the odds of an increase had roughly tripled inside two weeks following a jump in oil prices. FactSet records the average oil price in the second quarter of 2026 at $92.55, some 45% above the same quarter a year earlier. Ben Snider had already flagged in May that higher energy prices implied less rate relief than his team assumed entering the year, and Yardeni has stated his own projection assumes bond yields steady rather than rising.

              Sectors this framing usually involves: Energy, Utilities, Real Estate and Financials, alongside any sector valued on profits expected far in the future.

              CME FedWatch derived probabilities as reported by The Motley Fool, 27 Jul 2026, 6:30pm ET, and CNBC, 23 Jul 2026. These probabilities change continuously and this is a snapshot. Oil price data: FactSet Earnings Insight, 24 Jul 2026.

              Framework three: the capital spending path

              This framing asks whether the enormous sums being spent on artificial intelligence infrastructure will earn a return, and it is genuinely contested. Torsten Slok of Apollo wrote on 9 July 2026 that a slower payoff would mean disappointing cash flows as "committed capex and heavy depreciation hit on schedule," and that the effect would not stay contained within technology. Jim Chanos compared the buildout to the telecom expansion of 1999 and 2000 in comments reported on 17 June 2026. On the other side, strategists at BCA Research argued in a debate reported on 20 July 2026 that computing capacity remains scarce rather than oversupplied, that cloud order backlogs grew by roughly $750bn in two quarters, and that returns on this spending are being underestimated. JPMorgan's mid year outlook, summarised on 25 June 2026, put total global spending at $5.5tn through 2030 while noting the cycle is concentrated in a small group of companies.

              Sectors this framing usually involves: Information Technology, Utilities and Industrials.

              Torsten Slok, Apollo Global Management, 9 Jul 2026. Jim Chanos, reported 17 Jun 2026. BCA Research, reported by MarketWatch, 20 Jul 2026. JPMorgan Global Research mid year outlook, reported 25 Jun 2026.

              What one broker's published ratings imply

              Charles Schwab publishes a monthly view on all eleven sectors. As of 26 June 2026, using Schwab's own five level scale, the sectors it placed in its more favourable band were Communication Services, Health Care, Industrials and Materials. It placed Consumer Staples, Energy, Financials and Information Technology in the neutral band. It placed Utilities in its less favoured band and Consumer Discretionary and Real Estate in its least favoured band. Schwab changed its rating scale in March 2026, so these labels differ from those it published in earlier years. No July update had been posted when this report was compiled.

              This is reported as Schwab's published view on that date. It is not this report's view, and other firms disagree with parts of it.

              Read this before you use anything above

              • These are frameworks, not forecasts. They describe how named people are organising their thinking, which is a different thing from knowing what will happen.
              • The views quoted here change monthly, and the people holding them have been wrong before. Several of the figures cited were revised more than once during the six months this report covers.
              • Where sources conflict they have been left in conflict. The capital spending debate in framework three has credible, well resourced people on both sides reaching opposite conclusions from overlapping data.
              • Any historical pattern in this report rests on a very small sample. Three periods of sector leadership is not enough to establish that anything tends to happen.
              • Past performance does not indicate future results.
              Section 11

              What this data tells us

              The clearest pattern in the first half of 2026 is that the headline index number hid an unusually wide spread beneath it. The S&P 500 returned +10.21%, but the eleven sectors ranged from +20.20% for Industrials to -1.18% for Financials, a spread of more than twenty one percentage points. Smaller companies did considerably better than large ones, with the Russell 2000 up +22.57% against the S&P 500's ten. Three sectors finished within a whisker of each other at the top, Industrials, Information Technology and Energy, and they got there for entirely unrelated reasons.

              The second pattern is that the market as a whole became cheaper on the most commonly quoted valuation measure while going up. The S&P 500's forward P/E fell from 22.0 to 20.1 because expected profits were revised up faster than prices rose. Whether that is reassuring depends entirely on whether those revised profit expectations are met, and a meaningful part of the upward revision traces to one accounting item at one company rather than to broad based improvement.

              The third pattern is concentration, and it cuts in more than one direction. Information Technology alone is 36.83% of the index, so anyone holding a broad US index fund holds far more technology than they may realise. Yet within that sector the median company sat 29.4% below its 52 week high in late July even after a strong six months, and equal weighting the index would have produced a better return than market weighting it over the period. Concentration meant something different at the sector level than it did at the company level.

              Different readers will notice different things in the same table. Someone drawn to companies that look inexpensive relative to expected profits will note that Energy sits at the bottom of the forward P/E range while having delivered one of the strongest returns, and will want to know whether that reflects a durable change or an oil price that has already moved a great deal. Someone focused on income will look at the gap between price and total returns and see that dividends did more of the work in some sectors than others: Real Estate returned +9.70% on price alone against +11.52% including dividends, and Utilities +6.20% against +7.69%, while in Information Technology the two figures sat far closer together at +19.40% and +19.76%. Someone attentive to risk will note that the three sectors with the highest internal concentration, Energy at 73.47% in its largest ten holdings, Communication Services at 68.73% and Consumer Discretionary at 67.07%, are ones where a handful of company specific outcomes will determine the result. Someone watching the interest rate debate will note that the sectors most sensitive to borrowing costs sit on both sides of the performance table, which suggests rates were not the only thing driving returns over this period. None of these observations points to an action, and this report does not suggest one.
