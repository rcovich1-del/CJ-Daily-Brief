4 Wheel 101

The wheel is one loop, run over and over on a stock you are willing to own. Each step collects premium; the risk is that you end up holding a stock that keeps falling.

1 Sell a cash-secured put

Pick a stock you would genuinely be happy to own, and a strike below the current price. Sell the put and set aside the full strike × 100 in cash. You keep the premium either way. If the stock stays above the strike, the put expires worthless and you repeat step 1.

2 Get assigned

If the stock closes below your strike, you buy 100 shares at the strike. Your real cost basis is the strike minus the premium you already collected — which is why the wheel is often framed as "getting paid to place a limit order."

3 Sell a covered call

Now that you own 100 shares, sell a call above your cost basis. More premium. Selling below your cost basis converts the wheel into a locked-in loss if the shares get called, so the strike choice matters more here than anywhere else in the loop.

4 Shares called away — repeat

If the stock finishes above the call strike, your shares are sold at that strike. You have collected premium at every step plus any gain from cost basis to strike. Cash is free again, so you go back to step 1.

Reading the columns

Delta
Rough odds the option finishes in the money. A 0.25-delta put is loosely a one-in-four chance of assignment. Higher delta = more premium and more chance you end up owning the stock.
Premium / Bid
Per share. Multiply by 100 for one contract. This page uses the bid because that is what a seller realistically gets hit at, not the midpoint.
Cost basis
Strike minus premium collected — what you effectively pay per share if the put is assigned.
Return / Annualized
Premium against capital committed. The annualized figure extrapolates a short holding period out to a year and will always look large; it is a comparison tool between contracts, not a forecast of yearly income.
If called
Total return on a covered call if the shares get sold at the strike: premium plus the move from today's price up to the strike.
POP
Probability of profit at expiration, as the model sees it. Model output, not a guarantee.
IV rank
Where current implied volatility sits against its own past year. Higher IV rank means options are expensive relative to normal — generally better for sellers, though usually there is a reason they are expensive.
Open interest
Contracts outstanding. Thin open interest means wide spreads and a hard time closing early.
Earnings
Not shown on this page — you have to look it up. An earnings date falling before the contract expires is a binary event inside the trade's life, and the single most common way a calm wheel position turns into an unwanted large position overnight. Check it on every name.

Where the wheel actually hurts

  • It is not free income. A cash-secured put has essentially the same risk profile as owning the stock below the strike. If the stock drops 40%, you own it 40% lower, and the premium covers a fraction of that.
  • Capped upside, uncapped downside. The covered call caps your gain at the strike while the shares can keep falling. Running the loop on a stock in a real downtrend is how accounts get quietly ground down.
  • The highest annualized numbers are the most dangerous rows. Very high premium means the market expects a large move. That premium is compensation for risk, not a mispricing.
  • Only wheel what you want to own. Every filter on this page is secondary to that.
