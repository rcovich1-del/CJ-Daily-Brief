7Strategy in Focus — The Iron Condor

Why this one, today. The regime is caution and the weekday rotation lands on a neutral structure. That happens to fit the tape unusually well: SPY sits 0.07% under its 20-day and QQQ 0.46% under its own, RSI is near 50 on both, volume is running at half its average, and the VIX term structure is in clean contango with nothing priced for near-term event risk. A market with no direction and no volume is a market that pays you for selling the range instead of picking a side.

How it is built

An iron condor is two vertical credit spreads on the same underlying and expiration — a bull put spread below the market and a bear call spread above it. You collect a credit up front and keep all of it if the underlying finishes between your two short strikes.

  • Expiration: 30–45 days to expiry. This is where time decay accelerates without leaving you exposed to the wild gamma of the final week.
  • Short strikes: roughly 16 delta on each side — approximately one standard deviation out, implying about a 68% chance the underlying stays inside the range at expiry, before any management.
  • Long wings: 4–5% of the underlying's price beyond each short strike. The wings are what convert an undefined-risk strangle into a position with a known maximum loss.
  • Credit target: roughly one-third of the wing width. If your wings are $5 wide, you want about $1.65 in credit; take less and the reward stops justifying the tail.
  • Underlying selection: IV rank at or above 40%, no earnings inside the life of the trade, liquid options. Both GDX and EIX in Section 6 were flagged for exactly this structure.

A worked example in percentage and delta terms

Take a $100 underlying with an IV rank near 55% and no earnings for two months. Sell the 16-delta put roughly 8% below spot and the 16-delta call roughly 8% above. Buy wings 5% beyond each short — so about 13% below and 13% above. Your profit zone is the middle 16% of the distribution. On $5-wide wings you would look for roughly $1.60–$1.70 of credit, which puts maximum profit at about 48% of maximum loss.

Management: the standard discipline is to close at 50% of maximum profit rather than holding to expiration, and to exit or roll at 21 days to expiry regardless of profit and loss. Holding a condor into its final three weeks is where most of the strategy's historical losses come from — gamma rises sharply and a move that was harmless at 40 DTE becomes unrecoverable at 7.

The main risk

An iron condor is short gamma and short vega, and both of those work against you in the same event. The structural problem is the payoff asymmetry: on a $5-wide condor collecting $1.65, you risk $3.35 to make $1.65 — roughly two dollars at risk for every one you can win. That arithmetic only survives if you win far more often than you lose, which means a single trending move through a short strike can erase four or five clean months. The second, less obvious risk is the volatility environment itself. With the VIX at 15.21 — near the low end of its own 52-week range at the 8th percentile — you are selling volatility that is already cheap in absolute terms. Vol at 15 has far more room to expand than to compress, so an unexpected shock hits your short vega hard while simultaneously moving price against a short strike. That is why underlying selection insists on relative richness (IV rank) rather than merely high implied volatility, why the wings are non-negotiable, and why position size on any single condor should be small enough that the maximum loss is genuinely survivable. Assume you will take max loss on some of them, because you will.
