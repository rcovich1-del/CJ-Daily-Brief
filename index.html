7Strategy in Focus

Bear Call Spread Defined risk Bearish / neutral-to-lower Credit

Why this one, today. The regime is defense and it is a Thursday — a short-dated, defined-risk credit structure with a bearish-to-neutral bias is the rotation's match. More importantly it fits the actual conditions: implied volatility is elevated enough to make selling premium worthwhile (front VIX +8.7%), the term structure is still in contango so there is no crash bid distorting the curve, and the long-put screens came back empty because downside is already expensive. When protection is pricey, you want to be the one collecting for it — with a wing bought, not naked.

How it is built — in delta and percentage terms

Take a $100 stock in a sector that has just rolled over, with 30–45 days to the chosen expiry:

  • Sell the call at roughly the 25 delta — about 6–8% above spot in a 45%-IV name. That delta corresponds to roughly a 75% chance the option finishes worthless.
  • Buy the call about 5% further out (near the 10 delta) as the wing. This caps the loss and is the entire reason the structure is defined-risk.
  • Target a credit near one-third of the spread width. On a $5-wide spread that is about $1.65 collected, $3.35 at risk — roughly a 1:2 reward-to-risk on a trade that wins if the stock does anything except rally.

The position profits in three of four outcomes: the stock falls, goes sideways, or rises modestly but stays below the short call. Time decay works in your favour every day, and a decline in implied volatility helps as well. Most traders manage at roughly 50% of the maximum credit rather than holding to expiry, which cuts the tail exposure substantially.

The main risk — and it is not symmetric. A bear call spread's worst case is a sharp rally through both strikes, which loses the full width less the credit received — on the example above, roughly twice what was collected. The structure is short gamma: the loss accelerates as the stock approaches the short strike, so the position gets worse at an increasing rate exactly when you would most like it to stabilise. In today's tape the specific danger is a relief-rally squeeze: if Friday's CPI comes in soft, the ~70% rate-hike probability unwinds violently, and the most beaten-up names — precisely the ones a defense regime steers you toward shorting — rip hardest. Short interest is elevated in the same materials and semiconductor names that screen best. Size positions so that the maximum loss on the full spread width is acceptable, not so that the expected credit looks attractive, and treat the short strike as a decision point rather than a hope.

9Bottom Line

Four straight down days have taken the S&P 500 to 7,591.79 and put SPY five cents under its 50-day moving average — enough, under the rules, to move the regime to defense. The headline losses are small; the internals are not. Only about a third of U.S. issues advanced, new lows outnumbered new highs roughly six-to-one on the NYSE and nine-to-one on Nasdaq, and nine of eleven sectors closed red. The cause is coherent rather than mysterious: oil above $100 on the U.S.–Iran conflict, PPI accelerating to 5.4% year-over-year, every Treasury tenor at a 52-week high, and a market now assigning roughly 70% odds to a Fed hike next week. Volatility has responded but not panicked — VIX 17.84 in the moderate zone, term structure still in contango, put/call at a complacent 0.70.

That combination — elevated but orderly vol, deteriorating breadth, intact long-term trend — argues for defined-risk credit structures with a bearish or neutral tilt in the sectors where the damage is confirmed, at reduced size, and for leaving the energy complex alone entirely while geopolitical headlines can gap it through any reasonable expected move.

Top focus for Friday: the 8:30am CPI print. Core at +0.2% likely means a Fed hold and a relief rally that would punish fresh bearish exposure; core at +0.3% or higher likely means a hike is delivered next week and this four-day slide becomes something larger. Nothing else on the calendar matters by comparison — position sizing before the number is the entire decision.
