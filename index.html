7Strategy in Focus

Selected by regime and weekday. VIX at 14.25 with SPY 2.66% above its 20-day puts us in a normal regime, which permits a bullish bias. Monday's featured structure: the bull put spread.

Bull put spread — defined-risk short premium with a directional tilt

A bull put spread sells a put below the current price and buys a further out-of-the-money put underneath it. You collect a net credit and keep it in full if the stock finishes above your short strike. The long put you bought caps the loss — that is the entire point, and it is what separates this from selling a naked put.

Worked example — in delta and percentage terms

Take a $100 stock with an IV Rank near 60% and no earnings for six weeks.

  • Sell the 25-delta put — roughly 7% below spot, around $93, expiring in 35 days.
  • Buy the 15-delta put — roughly 12% below spot, around $88, same expiry.
  • Spread width $5. A typical credit at these deltas is $1.50–$1.75, or about 30–35% of width.
  • Maximum profit the credit, $1.50, kept if the stock closes above $93.
  • Maximum loss width minus credit — $5.00 − $1.50 = $3.50, realised only if the stock closes below $88.
  • Breakeven short strike minus credit = $91.50, or 8.5% below spot.
  • Rough probability of profit the 25-delta short put implies roughly a 75% chance of finishing above it. You are risking $3.50 to make $1.50 — a 2.3-to-1 loss-to-gain ratio — in exchange for winning about three times in four.
The main risk

The payoff is asymmetric against you, and that asymmetry is the whole risk. In the example above you win $1.50 about 75% of the time and lose $3.50 about 25% of the time — expectancy that is only slightly positive at best, and negative the moment the market's implied probabilities are right and your fills are worse than mid. A single maximum loss erases more than two winners. That arithmetic breaks in exactly one circumstance: a fast, large gap lower, where the stock jumps past both strikes and you take the full loss with no chance to manage. The current environment makes this specifically relevant. With VIX at 14.25 — the 3.97 percentile of its 52-week range — you are being paid historically little to accept that gap risk, while the put/call ratio at 0.66 says few people are hedged. Cheap index volatility is not the same as low realised risk; it is the price of insurance when nobody wants any. If you run this structure now, run it smaller than you would at VIX 20, keep the long wing genuinely close enough to matter, and treat the maximum loss as a number you will actually pay, not a theoretical bound.

8Game Plan

Setting up for Monday, August 17.

Backdrop. Three straight weekly gains, a record S&P close on Thursday and a record Russell close on Friday, with two-thirds of the index above its 50-day. Volatility is at the floor of its yearly range and the term structure is steeply upward-sloping. The bullish case is broad participation and a Fed that no longer looks likely to tighten in September. The bearish case is a consumer that just posted a 0.6% drop in retail sales heading straight into the biggest retail earnings week of the quarter, with light volume and complacent hedging behind the advance. Monday itself is thin — two liquid reporters and no scheduled data of consequence — so the session is likely to be about positioning ahead of Home Depot on Tuesday.

Favor

  • Defined-risk credit in single names with elevated IV Rank — index vol is too cheap to sell, but stock-level IV Rank in the 40–70% band still pays.
  • Energy exposure — the only sector with real momentum on both timeframes, and the catalyst (Hormuz) is unresolved rather than spent.
  • The broadening trade — small caps and equal-weight are at the top of their ranges, not the mega-cap complex.
  • Shorter tenors — a steep contango means the front of the curve is where premium decays fastest.

Avoid

  • Selling index premium — at VIX 14.25 and $VXST 10.61 you are underwriting the tail for almost nothing.
  • New positions in the retail complex before their prints — HD Tue, TGT/LOW/TJX Wed, WMT Thu, with Consumer Discretionary already the weakest sector of the week.
  • Naked short puts — see Section 7. If you want the premium, buy the wing.
  • Chasing the tech complex — QQQ's 20-day still sits below its 50-day and Friday's leadership was elsewhere.
  • Oversizing on light-volume strength — Friday ran 56–67% of normal volume across all four index proxies.

9Bottom Line

The market goes into Monday in a normal regime and with the strongest internals it has shown in months: three consecutive weekly gains, a record close on the S&P Thursday and on the Russell Friday, roughly two-thirds of the index above both its 20- and 50-day averages, and equal-weight RSP at 99.5% of its 52-week range. This is a broad advance, not a narrow one, and softer CPI and PPI have removed the near-term threat of another Fed tightening. That combination deserves respect.

What it does not deserve is complacency. Friday's session ran at roughly 60–67% of normal volume, the put/call ratio at 0.66 sits a quarter below its 20-day average, and VIX at 14.25 is at the fourth percentile of its yearly range. Meanwhile July retail sales fell 0.6% against an expected rise, consumer sentiment weakened, and Consumer Discretionary was the only sector negative on both the day and the week — heading directly into a calendar where Home Depot, Target, Lowe's, TJX and Walmart all report. Cheap volatility with an unresolved consumer question is a specific kind of setup: not a reason to be short, but a strong reason not to be short volatility at index level.

Top focus for Monday: Monday's own calendar is nearly empty, so use it to size and structure rather than to chase. The single most consequential decision this week is how much retail-sector and consumer-discretionary exposure you carry into Tuesday's Home Depot print — the answer should be set before the open on Monday, not after it.
