7Strategy in Focus

Bullish Diagonal Spread Selected for: Normal regime · Wednesday

A bullish diagonal buys a longer-dated, deeper in-the-money call and sells a shorter-dated, further out-of-the-money call against it. It sits between a long call and a covered call: you get directional exposure from the long leg while the short leg pays you to wait. It is the structure that fits today's conditions because the regime is constructive (VIX 14.55, SPY 3.26% above its 50-day, 10 of 12 indicator gauges risk-on) but the two cautions on the board — index volume at 0.63–0.69× normal and a 0.74 put/call ratio — argue against paying full price for pure directional exposure.

Worked example, in delta and percentage terms

Take a $100 stock in an established uptrend with an IV Rank near 35% — low enough that the long leg is not overpriced.

  • Long leg: buy a call about 75 days out at roughly 70 delta, which lands near a $92–94 strike. It costs something like 11–13% of the share price and carries most of the position's directional exposure.
  • Short leg: sell a call about 21 days out at roughly 28 delta, near $107 — about 7% above spot. It brings in perhaps 2.0–2.5% of the share price.
  • Net debit: roughly 9–11% of share price, versus 11–13% for the naked long call. The short leg has funded about a fifth of the position.
  • Net delta: roughly 70 − 28 = 42 delta. You participate in about 42 cents of every dollar the stock moves at the outset, rising as the short leg decays.
  • Net theta: close to flat or slightly positive at entry, because the 21-day short option decays faster than the 75-day long option. That is the entire point — you are not bleeding while you wait.
  • Roll: when the short call reaches roughly 21 days to expiry or 50–70% of its maximum value, buy it back and sell the next cycle at the same 25–30 delta. Each roll lowers your effective cost basis.
  • Best outcome: the stock grinds up toward but not through the short strike over several cycles. Two or three successful rolls can retire a meaningful share of the original debit.
Main risk — and it is not the one most people name. The obvious worry is a sharp drop, and yes, a gap lower costs you: at 42 net delta a 10% decline takes roughly 4% of share price out of the position immediately, with the long leg's vega offering only partial cushion because falling markets raise implied volatility. But the more common way this trade disappoints is the opposite — a fast rally straight through the short strike. Above $107 in the example, your short call gains value roughly one-for-one with the stock while your 70-delta long gains less than that, so upside is capped, and you can be assigned early on the short leg if it goes deep in the money near a dividend. You end up right about direction and still underperform simply owning the shares. There is also a subtler exposure: the diagonal is long vega on the back month and short vega on the front. If the term structure flattens — front-month volatility rising toward the back — the structure loses on both legs at once. With VXST at 11.09 against VXMT at 20.82, the curve is about as steep as it gets, which means the flattening risk sits squarely against this position. Size it as though a 10% adverse gap is a normal outcome, because in a market running on 0.63× volume it is.
