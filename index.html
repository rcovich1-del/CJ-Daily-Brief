07 Strategy in Focus

Selected by regime and weekday. Normal regime, Tuesday — and with index volatility at the bottom of its range, long premium is comparatively cheap, so the debit side gets the spotlight.

Bull Call Spread (long call vertical / debit spread)
Debit Bullish — matches Normal regime

Why this, today. A bull call spread buys one call and sells a higher-strike call in the same expiry. You pay a net debit, cap the upside at the short strike, and cap the loss at what you paid. The setup earns its place when two things are true at once — you have a directional bullish view, and options are not expensive. Both hold: the regime is Normal, and VIX at 15.45 sits at the 9.4th percentile of its own 52-week range with the curve in normal contango. When you buy a naked call in a cheap-vol tape you still pay full freight for time; the short leg is what makes the position affordable, and its cost is the upside you agree to give up.

Educational example — illustrative stock at $100, 45 days to expiry
  • Buy the call closest to 60 delta — roughly 2% in the money. This is the leg carrying the directional exposure; a 60-delta long behaves like about 60 shares at the outset.
  • Sell the call closest to 30 delta — roughly 6% out of the money, same expiry. This is your financing and your ceiling.
  • Net debit in this delta pairing typically prices around 35–45% of the spread width. On an 8%-wide spread, expect to pay roughly 3% of the underlying's price.
  • Max profit = spread width minus debit, realised only if the stock closes at or above the short strike. Max loss = the debit, in full, if the stock closes below the long strike.
  • Breakeven ≈ long strike + debit — call it 5% above spot in this example. That is the honest hurdle: the stock has to move, and move within 45 days.
  • Net delta at entry is roughly 30 (60 long minus 30 short), so the position is materially less sensitive than owning stock — sizing off notional will over-size it.
Main risk

The debit is fully at risk, and a stalled stock loses on more fronts than most people expect. A bull call spread needs direction and timing. If the underlying simply goes nowhere for 45 days, the position expires worthless below the long strike and you lose 100% of what you paid — there is no partial credit for being directionally right eventually. The subtler trap is volatility. A long call at least benefits if implied vol expands; a vertical largely does not, because the short leg gains value alongside the long one and net vega is small. So the low-vol entry that made the trade cheap gives you no rescue mechanism if the thesis is wrong — you cannot count on a volatility pop to bail out a bad directional call. And in a cheap-vol tape the standing temptation is to widen the spread for a bigger payoff; that raises the debit, pushes the breakeven further away, and forces a larger move just to return to flat. Size the position against the entire debit as your loss, not against a stop level, because a gap through the long strike can leave nothing to stop out of.
