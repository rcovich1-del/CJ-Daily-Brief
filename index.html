07Strategy in Focus

The Bull Put Spread
Bullish / neutral bias Normal regime · Thursday

Why this one, today. The defining feature of this tape is a split between index volatility and single-name volatility. VIX closed at 14.63 and the 9-day at 11.37 — index premium is nearly worthless. Meanwhile today's credit screens are full of names carrying IV Ranks between 50% and 90%. That gap is dispersion: individual stocks are being priced for movement the index is not. A defined-risk credit spread in a single name is the structure that harvests it, and it is the structure the regime permits — normal, with SPY above every moving average and the trend intact on all three timeframes.

How it is built

Sell one put at roughly 20–25 delta — on a 40–50% implied-vol name at 30–45 DTE, that lands about 8–12% below spot.
Buy one put further out at roughly 10 delta to cap the loss.
Width between the strikes: typically 5–10% of spot.
Target credit: 25–33% of the width. A $10-wide spread should pay $2.50–$3.30.
Max profit: the credit, kept in full if the stock finishes above the short strike.
Max loss: width minus credit — on that $10 spread paying $3.00, the loss is $7.00.
Management: close at 50% of max credit, or at 21 DTE, whichever comes first.

A worked example, in percentages

Take a $100 stock in an established uptrend, above its 20- and 50-day, implied vol at 45%, IV Rank 60%, no earnings for two months. At 35 DTE the 20-delta put sits near $90 and the 10-delta near $85. Selling the 90 and buying the 85 gives a $5-wide spread that should collect around $1.50, or 30% of width. The stock can fall 10% and the trade still pays in full. Return on risk is $1.50 against $3.50 at stake — about 43% if held to expiry, and roughly 21% if closed at the 50% target, which is where most of these should be taken off.

The main risk

A bull put spread has capped loss but badly asymmetric payoff — that $5 spread risks $3.50 to make $1.50, so it loses roughly 2.3× what it wins. The danger is not a slow drift lower; it is a gap. Between the short and long strikes the position is short gamma, and in a fast move down the short put gains delta far quicker than the long put you own beneath it, so the mark-to-market loss arrives before the defined risk provides any comfort. Today's environment sharpens this specifically: with VIX at 14.63 and the 9-day at 11.37, the market is priced for calm, and a single macro surprise re-rates every short put in a portfolio simultaneously — correlation goes to one exactly when diversification was the plan. There is also a behavioural trap. A 20-delta short put has roughly an 80% probability of expiring worthless, so the strategy wins far more often than it loses, and a long run of small wins builds conviction that has nothing to do with edge. Position size to the maximum loss and to the number of correlated positions held at once — never to the win rate.
