7 Strategy in Focus

Bull Put Spread
NORMAL REGIME WEDNESDAY ROTATION BULLISH BIAS — MATCHES REGIME

A bull put spread sells one put and buys another further out of the money in the same expiry. It is a defined-risk credit trade: money comes in at the open, maximum profit is that credit, maximum loss is the width of the strikes minus the credit. It is the natural structure for the conditions on this page — a normal regime with VIX at 14.89, index prices above their moving averages, and a scattering of individual names carrying IV ranks in the 60th to 100th percentile. You want to be selling that dispersion, not the index vol, which is cheap.

Worked example — in delta and percentage terms only

Take a $150 stock with a 70% IV rank and no earnings for two months. Choose an expiry 30–45 days out — far enough that time decay is meaningful but not so far that the position is dead money. Sell the put closest to 25 delta, which will sit roughly 6–8% below spot at that vol. Buy the put roughly 5% below that strike to define the risk. On a spread of that width you would typically collect a credit near 30–35% of the width. That credit is the maximum gain; the width minus the credit is the maximum loss, so the structure risks about two dollars to make one.

The 25-delta short strike is approximately the market's estimate that the trade finishes fully profitable about 75% of the time. A common management rule is to close at 50% of maximum credit rather than holding to expiry, which sacrifices the last of the decay in exchange for cutting the time spent exposed to a gap — and it takes the position off before gamma risk accelerates in the final two weeks.

The main risk

The risk-reward is inverted, and that is the whole point to understand before using this structure. Collecting a third of the width means roughly two dollars are at risk for every one that can be made. The trade wins often and loses rarely, but each loss is about twice the size of each win — so a single bad outcome erases two or three good ones, and a run of them erases a season. That maths only works if position sizing is set so that the maximum loss on any one spread is tolerable, because in a fast decline both strikes go in the money together and the spread reaches full loss with no meaningful chance to manage out. Assignment is a second, separate hazard: the short put can be exercised early, most often around a dividend, leaving stock to be dealt with before the long leg has done anything for you. And the environment that makes premium look most attractive — high IV rank — usually reflects genuine uncertainty about the underlying rather than a gift. Selling volatility is not the same as being right about direction, and this structure requires being right about direction.
