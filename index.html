7Strategy in Focus — The Bear Call Spread

Why this one, today. The regime is Defense, and it is Defense on price rather than on fear: the volatility index at 17.20 is below the 18 threshold, but the broad index has lost its 50-day average and the Nasdaq-100 fund has lost both its 20- and its 50-day. Under the rule that the more severe condition governs, that is Defense whatever the volatility index says. A defined-risk structure that profits from a market that falls, drifts, or simply fails to recover through a level it has already lost is the expression that matches that reading — and unlike a long put, it is paid rather than paying, which matters when the front of the volatility curve has already been bid up 16.2% in five sessions ahead of a decision. It is a credit structure, so the standards below are the vertical-spread standards.

How it is built

A bear call spread is a single vertical credit spread placed above the market — sell a call, buy a further-out call of the same expiration as the wing. Two legs, defined risk, and a maximum loss that is known before the trade is on.

LegActionApprox. deltaApprox. distance from spot
Short call (the leg that collects)Sell≈ 15–20+5% to +8%
Long call (the wing that caps the loss)Buy≈ 8–10+8% to +11%

Worked example — in percentages, not strikes

Take a hypothetical $100 stock with an implied volatility rank around 60% and 45 days to expiration. Sell the roughly 17-delta call about 6% above spot, then buy the wing another 3% further out — a wing width of about 3% of spot, or $3. Suppose the net credit is $0.80 on that $3.00 wing. Then: maximum profit $0.80 (the credit, kept if price finishes below the short strike), maximum loss $2.20 (wing width minus credit), a breakeven roughly 6.8% above spot, and a credit that is 27% of the wing width. The roughly 17-delta short call implies about an 83% chance price finishes below it at expiration — and that is before any management.

The main risk — read this part twice

The payoff is asymmetric against you, and the direction of that asymmetry is the whole trade. In the example above you risk $2.20 to make $0.80 — you lose 2.75× what you win, and you need to be right roughly 73% of the time simply to break even before commissions. A structure that wins most months is not the same as a structure that makes money, and a single trade carried to maximum loss erases nearly three winners. The specific danger of a spread placed above the market is the upside gap: a bid takeover, a surprise guide, or a policy statement read as dovish can open the stock through the short strike overnight, with no opportunity in between to adjust or to close. That is why the earnings filter exists in Section 6, and why an elevated IV rank is required rather than merely welcome — you want to be paid for the gap risk you are taking on, not selling cheap calls on a quiet name. The subtler risk is the mirror of the obvious one: this is a short-volatility position, so if implied volatility expands after entry the mark moves against you even with price sitting still, and with a decision landing tomorrow that is the more likely of the two to happen first. Size the position on maximum loss rather than on credit received, and decide the exit before entering, not after the gap.
