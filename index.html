07 Strategy in Focus

The Cash-Secured Put

Selected by regime and weekday: a normal regime on a Thursday. The bias is neutral-to-mildly-bullish, which is consistent with a market still above all three of its major moving averages.
What it is

You sell a put option and simultaneously set aside enough cash to buy 100 shares at the strike if you are assigned. You collect the premium up front. If the stock stays above the strike through expiry, the option expires worthless and the premium is your return. If it falls below, you buy the shares at the strike — and your effective cost basis is the strike minus the premium you already collected. It is the entry half of the wheel, and it is the single structure where "I wanted to own this anyway" and "I want to get paid to wait" line up exactly.

A worked example, in delta and percentage terms

Take a $100 stock with 40% implied volatility, earnings cleared for at least two months, and no dividend inside the window. You sell the 35-day put at roughly the 20-delta strike, which sits about 7% below spot at $93. Suppose that put trades near $1.40, or 1.4% of spot.

The 20 delta is a useful shorthand: it says the market currently assigns roughly a 20% chance the option finishes in the money, so roughly an 80% chance you simply keep the premium. Your cash requirement is the full $9,300. The premium is $140, so the unlevered return on the cash set aside is about 1.5% over 35 days, which annualises to roughly 15.7% if you could repeat it uninterrupted — a large "if" that is doing a lot of work.

If the stock closes anywhere above $93, you keep the $140 and the trade is over. If it closes at $88, you are assigned at $93 but your effective basis is $91.60 after the premium, so you are down about 3.9% on the position rather than the 12% the stock itself fell. That cushion — the 7% out-of-the-money distance plus the 1.4% premium — is the entire economic point of the structure.

Sizing convention: because the cash is genuinely committed, position count should be governed by the assignment scenario, not the premium. A useful discipline is to ask whether you would be comfortable owning every one of your open short puts simultaneously, on the same bad day. If the answer is no, the book is too large.

The main risk

The payoff is fundamentally asymmetric and the asymmetry runs against you. Your maximum gain is the premium — $140 in the example above, capped, known on day one. Your maximum loss is the strike less the premium, all the way to zero: $9,160. You are exchanging a small, high-probability gain for a small-probability but very large loss, and that is a trade that looks brilliant for many consecutive months and then repays several years of premium in a single week.

Three specific ways it bites. First, gap risk: a stock that falls 30% overnight on a fraud disclosure or a failed trial gives you no opportunity to manage — there is no stop-loss that works across a gap. Second, correlation: short puts across ten names feel diversified until a broad market decline moves all ten against you at once, which is exactly when your cash is most needed elsewhere. Today's tape is a mild version of that — only two of eleven sectors closed green. Third, the ownership assumption is not optional: the structure is only defensible on an underlying you genuinely want to hold at the strike. Selling puts on a name purely because the premium is fat converts a considered position into a forced purchase of something you never analysed.

Worth naming plainly: in the current setup, implied volatility is only moderate (VIX 16.01) while breadth has just rolled over. That is the least attractive combination for a premium seller — ordinary compensation for above-ordinary downside. It argues for smaller size and for insisting on the earnings-cleared and relative-volume filters rather than relaxing them to find more candidates.

09 Bottom Line

A broad, orderly decline that did more damage to internals than to the headline. The S&P proxy fell 0.84% and the Dow 1.27%, but nine of eleven sectors closed red, the NYSE advance–decline difference swung nearly 1,000 issues negative, one-month lows overtook one-month highs, and the share of S&P names above their 20-day average dropped eight points in a session. The only genuine leadership was commodity-linked, with energy positive on both the day and the week behind a WTI settle at a one-month high on Iran escalation, and gold within a dollar of its own one-month high.

Top focus: whether SPY holds its 20-day average at 760.99. Spot is 0.21% above it — roughly a quarter of one average session's range — and it is the line that separates the current normal regime from caution. With breadth already negative, a close below that level would mean the internals were leading and the index was simply late to confirm.
