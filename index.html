07Strategy in Focus — Bull Put Spread

Why this strategy, today. The regime resolves to normal — VIX below 18, SPY above its 20-day and 50-day — so a structure with a neutral-to-bullish bias is consistent with the backdrop, which is the requirement the regime rules impose. Tuesday's slot in the weekday rotation lands on the defined-risk credit structure. And the screen supports it: half a dozen names cleared every filter with IV Rank above 50% and earnings out beyond the 14-day window, meaning there is genuine premium to sell that is not simply an earnings artifact.

What it is. A bull put spread sells one put and buys another put at a lower strike in the same expiry. You collect a net credit up front. The trade profits if the underlying stays above the higher (short) strike through expiry, and the long put below caps the loss. It is a bet that a stock will not fall below a chosen level — not a bet that it will rise. That distinction matters: it can win on a flat or even modestly declining tape, which is why it suits a normal regime with soft internals rather than a strongly trending one.

Educational example, in delta and percentage terms. Take a hypothetical $100 stock with IV Rank near 60% and no earnings inside 45 days:

  • Sell the ~25-delta put, typically 5–6% below spot. The 25-delta figure is a rough market-implied estimate that the option finishes in the money about a quarter of the time.
  • Buy the ~10-delta put, typically 11–12% below spot. This defines the maximum loss and reduces the margin requirement.
  • Expiry: 30–45 days out, where time decay accelerates without leaving excessive event exposure.
  • Credit: aim for roughly 30% of the width between the strikes. On a 6-point-wide spread, that is about 1.80 in credit.
  • Maximum profit: the credit received (1.80), realised if the stock finishes above the short strike.
  • Maximum loss: width minus credit (6.00 − 1.80 = 4.20), realised if the stock finishes below the long strike.
  • Breakeven: the short strike less the credit — roughly 7.5% below spot in this example.
  • Risk-reward: risking 4.20 to make 1.80. The trade needs to win materially more than two times out of three simply to break even before costs.
Main risk. The payoff is deliberately asymmetric in the wrong direction: you risk substantially more than you can make on any single position. In the example above, one maximum loss wipes out the gains from more than two maximum wins. That structure is survivable only if the win rate genuinely stays high and position sizes stay uniform — and the failure mode is precisely that a high win rate breeds confidence, sizes creep up, and one gap does disproportionate damage. Specific hazards today: a gap through the short strike leaves no opportunity to adjust, since the loss is realised before the market reopens; correlation risk means a broad market decline hits every open spread simultaneously, so six positions in a normal regime are not six independent bets; and short American-style puts carry early assignment risk once they move in the money, particularly around ex-dividend dates. Note too that with the VIX at 15.84, index-level premium is not generous — the credit per unit of risk is thinner than the strategy's reputation suggests, which is exactly when traders are tempted to widen strikes or add size to compensate. Both make the asymmetry worse. Defined risk means the loss is bounded, not that it is small.
