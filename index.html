07Strategy in Focus — The Bull Put Spread

Why this one, today. The regime reads normal — VIX at 14.53 is under the 18 threshold and SPY at 770.19 is above its 20-day at 769.05 — so a structure with a bullish-to-neutral bias is consistent with the tape. But index volatility sits in the bottom 5% of its own 52-week range, which means outright long calls are cheap and outright index premium sales pay almost nothing. The bull put spread threads that: it collects premium where premium actually exists (single names carrying IV ranks in the 48–85% band), it defines the loss in advance, and it does not require the market to go up — only to not fall through a level.

How it is built, in delta and percentage terms

  • Sell a put at roughly 20–25 delta. On the names above that lands somewhere below the option-implied expected move — for a 4% expected move, typically 5–7% under spot.
  • Buy a further out-of-the-money put roughly 3–4% below the short strike. That long leg is what converts an open-ended obligation into a fixed, known maximum loss.
  • Expiration: 30–45 days out. Shorter than 30 days and the position is dominated by gamma rather than time decay; longer than 45 and capital sits idle while decay is slow.
  • Credit target: around one third of the width between the strikes. Collect less than that and the risk-reward stops paying for the tail; demand much more and the short strike ends up too close to spot.
  • Management: close at roughly 50% of the maximum credit, or at about 21 days to expiration, whichever comes first. The last three weeks are where a spread that has behaved well can still turn.

The main risk — read this part twice

A bull put spread's maximum loss is the width of the strikes minus the credit received, which in practice means two to three times what was collected. The break-even sits at the short strike less the credit, and losses do not build gradually: if the underlying gaps below the long strike the position is at close to its full defined loss immediately, with no premium left to collect and very little time value to recover. Two specific hazards apply to this week. First, Monday is a holiday, so any position opened before the weekend carries three calendar days of headline exposure — an ongoing U.S.–Iran conflict, oil up 9.26% in five sessions — with no ability to adjust. Second, CPI and PPI land mid-week and are, on the consensus read, the deciding input on whether the Fed hikes at the September meeting; a hawkish surprise would hit exactly the kind of high-beta single names that carry the fattest IV ranks. The defined-risk structure caps what can be lost, but it does not make the loss unlikely, and selling premium into a genuinely two-sided macro event is a decision to be paid for taking that risk, not a way to avoid it.

09Bottom Line

A jobs report that beat by more than 100,000 pushed yields to 52-week highs at the front of the curve and left markets pricing a coin flip on a September rate hike — and the S&P still only gave back 0.38%. That is the whole session in one line: a hawkish repricing that equities largely absorbed. Beneath it, the tape was narrower than the index suggests. Top focus: the mid-week CPI and PPI prints. Waller's willingness to hold rates steady was explicitly conditioned on that data, hike odds are near 50%, and index volatility is priced as though nothing is coming. That combination — a genuinely binary macro event against the cheapest index vol of the year — is the single thing most likely to reprice this market, and it is the reason to keep structures defined and small into Tuesday's reopen.
