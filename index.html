7Strategy in Focus

Bull Put Spread
Bullish / Neutral Defined Risk Net Credit

Why this one, today. The rotation is driven by regime and weekday. Regime is normal — VIX at 15.19 is below the 18 threshold and SPY sits above both its 20-day and 50-day averages, so no severity rule overrides. Monday in a normal regime rotates to the bull put spread, and a moderately bullish structure is consistent with a regime that permits one. It also fits the tape mechanically: with front-month volatility depressed and the term structure in steep contango, an undefined short-premium position collects very little for the tail it accepts. Defining the risk costs some credit and buys a known worst case.

Educational Example — Delta & Percentage Terms
SellPut at roughly 25 delta, near 5–7% below spot
BuyPut roughly 4–5% lower (about 10 delta)
Expiry30–45 days — the region where time decay accelerates without gamma dominating
NetCredit, typically 20–35% of the strike width
Max profitThe credit, if the underlying finishes above the short strike
Max lossStrike width minus credit — known at entry
Approx. win rateAround 75% at 25 delta, before costs — the delta is a rough probability of finishing in the money
ManagementCommonly closed at 50% of maximum credit, or rolled/closed near 21 days to expiry

Numbers above are illustrative of the structure, not a recommendation on any security. Deltas and credits vary with the chain at the moment of execution.

Main Risk

The defining feature of this structure is that it wins small and often and loses large and rarely. At 25 delta the position finishes profitable roughly three times in four, but the loss on the fourth occasion is several multiples of any single win. The arithmetic is unforgiving: a spread collecting 30% of its width needs about a 77% win rate simply to break even before costs. A structure that feels reliable can be a losing proposition if the strike is placed carelessly or the position is sized as though the frequent small win were the whole distribution.

Two specific hazards apply to today's tape. First, entering short premium when VIX is at 15.19 and the 9-day is at 12.39 means selling volatility near the low end of its own range — the credit is small, and there is far more room for volatility to expand against the position than to contract in its favour. Second, a bull put spread is short volatility and long direction simultaneously, so a sharp decline hurts on both legs at once. That is exactly the scenario the breadth divergence in Section 1 is flagging as possible.

The defined long leg caps the loss, which is the entire reason to prefer this over a naked put in the current environment. What it does not do is make the loss small.

9Bottom Line

A quiet, narrow decline in a market whose trend is still fully intact. The four major indices lost between 0.16% and 0.49% on roughly 70% of normal volume, VIX fell to 15.19, and ten of twelve trend gauges across SPY and QQQ read risk-on with no risk-off signal anywhere. That is a normal regime by every rule, and the featured structure is calibrated to it.

What keeps it from being straightforwardly constructive is the disagreement underneath. Decliners beat advancers by 689 on the NYSE and 855 on the Nasdaq against half-percent index losses, one-month new highs contracted sharply, and the sectors carrying the damage were consumer and media rather than anything defensive — Communication Services −1.89% and Consumer Discretionary −1.23%, both also the week's worst. Energy stood alone in the green, +1.08%, on crude strength that has Brent near $90. Meanwhile the volatility complex is pricing essentially nothing: the 9-day at 12.39 sits 2.80 points below spot VIX and the put/call ratio has fallen to 0.77.

The 30-year Treasury at a 19-year high is the variable that ties the rest together. It explains the pressure in real estate, it complicates every retail print this week, and it is the most plausible route from a shallow, orderly pullback to something that actually moves volatility. Cheap volatility is not the same as low risk — it is a statement about what the market expects, not about what can happen.
