7Strategy in Focus

Caution regime · midweek · neutral bias

The Iron Condor

An iron condor sells an out-of-the-money put spread and an out-of-the-money call spread on the same underlying in the same expiration. It is a defined-risk, market-neutral position: the trader collects a net credit and keeps all of it if the underlying finishes anywhere between the two short strikes at expiration. It is the structure that fits today's reading — a tape drifting lower with deteriorating breadth but no panic, VIX at 16.46 in the moderate zone, and a futures curve still in contango. There is no directional edge to lean on and no volatility spike to fade, which is exactly the environment a range-bound premium sale is designed for.

Worked example, in delta and percentage terms. Take an underlying with an IV Rank near 45% and roughly 45 days to expiration. Sell the put at approximately 16 delta and the call at approximately 16 delta — that places both short strikes near the edges of a one-standard-deviation move, historically around 8–10% away from spot on a mid-volatility name. Buy protective wings roughly 5% further out on each side, which caps the loss on either wing. On a structure like that the net credit typically lands near 25–35% of the width between a short strike and its wing, giving a maximum loss of roughly two to three times the credit received. Common management: close at 50% of maximum profit rather than holding to expiration, and act on the tested side when a short strike reaches roughly 30 delta — either rolling that side out in time or closing the whole position.

The main risk. An iron condor has a high probability of a small win and a low probability of a large loss, and the loss side is where the strategy actually lives or dies. Because both wings are capped, the worst case is bounded — but that worst case is several times the credit collected, so a single trend day through one side can erase many successful cycles. Two specific hazards apply right now. First, gap risk: the credit assumes the underlying stays in range, and an overnight headline — a further oil escalation, an unexpected Fed outcome next week — can open the underlying beyond a short strike with no opportunity to manage. Second, correlation risk: condors placed across several names in a caution regime feel diversified but are not, because in a genuine market-wide move every position tests the same side at the same time. The structure sells the tails; the tails are precisely what a geopolitically driven, rate-sensitive tape is currently threatening to deliver.
