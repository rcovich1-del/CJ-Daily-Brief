07 Strategy in Focus

Normal regime · Thursday rotation

The Bull Put Spread (Short Put Vertical)

A defined-risk credit structure: sell a put below the current price, buy a further out-of-the-money put underneath it for protection, and collect the difference. It profits if the stock rises, goes sideways, or falls only modestly — and the maximum loss is known before the order is sent.

Why it fits tonight

The regime is normal, which permits a bullish bias. VIX at 14.51 means index-level premium is thin, but single-name IV ranks are elevated across the post-earnings cohort — CRWD at 52%, HPQ at 50%, MPC at 74%. That split is exactly what a vertical exploits: it sells the rich single-name premium while capping the tail with a long leg that is cheap in a low-index-vol world. Selling a naked put in this environment collects a similar credit with a materially worse loss profile.

Worked example — in deltas and percentages

Take a $100 stock with an IV rank near 50 and no earnings for two months.

Sell the put at roughly 25 delta — about 6–8% below spot for a 35-day expiry.
Buy the put roughly 5% further down, near 10–12 delta, to define the spread width.
Target credit: about one third of the width. On a $5-wide spread that is roughly $1.65 collected, leaving $3.35 at risk.

Max profit is the credit, earned if the stock is above the short strike at expiry.
Max loss is width minus credit, roughly 2x the credit.
Breakeven sits about 8–10% below today's price.
Approximate probability of profit at a 25-delta short strike is around 75% — the market is paying about 2-to-1 odds against a roughly 3-to-1 chance, which is the whole trade.

Main risk — read this part twice

The payoff is asymmetric in the wrong direction. A bull put spread wins often and small, and loses rarely and large: roughly two dollars at risk for every one collected in the example above. Four winners can be erased by one loser. That arithmetic only works if position size is set so that the maximum loss — not the expected loss — is survivable, and if the trader actually accepts the loss rather than rolling it down and out indefinitely, which converts a defined-risk trade into an undefined one.

Two specific ways it breaks tonight. First, gap risk: the long leg caps the loss but does not prevent it, and a stock that gaps 15% below the short strike delivers the full maximum loss instantly with no chance to manage. Second, correlation: with only 44% of S&P members above their 20-day average and a single sector carrying the index, a basket of bull put spreads across CRWD, NOW, BUG and TENB is not four independent trades — it is one leveraged bet on software and cybersecurity holding up. A market-wide risk-off event, of which Jackson Hole tomorrow is a live candidate, would hit all of them at once. The defined risk is per position; the correlated risk is not.

A low VIX also means the long protective leg is cheap, which is a genuine advantage — but a 14.51 VIX is a reading, not a promise. Volatility is mean-reverting from both directions, and the 6-month VIX at 20.36 is telling you the market itself does not expect 14.51 to persist.
