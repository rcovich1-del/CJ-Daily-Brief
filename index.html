7Strategy in Focus — The Iron Condor

Why this one, today. The regime is Caution: VIX at 17.10 is below the 18 threshold, but SPY closed under its 20-day while holding its 50-day by just 0.26%, and a Fed decision lands Wednesday with a hike close to fully priced. The day's selling was violent in chips and cybersecurity rallied just as hard in the opposite direction — large single-name moves inside an index that barely moved. That is a market rotating rather than trending, waiting on a known event — the specific condition a neutral, defined-risk, premium-collecting structure is designed for. A directional structure in either direction is making a call the tape is not supporting. The condor makes no call at all; it simply requires that price stays inside a band.

How it is built

An iron condor is two credit spreads on the same underlying and expiration — a bull put spread below the price and a bear call spread above it. Four legs, all defined risk.

LegActionTypical deltaApprox. distance from spot
Long put (lower wing)Buy≈ 8–10−9% to −11%
Short putSell≈ 15–20−6% to −8%
Short callSell≈ 15–20+6% to +8%
Long call (upper wing)Buy≈ 8–10+9% to +11%

Worked example — in percentages, not strikes

Take a hypothetical $100 stock with an IV rank around 55% and 40 days to expiration. Sell the roughly 17-delta put about 7% below spot and the roughly 17-delta call about 7% above spot, then buy the 9-delta wings another 3% out on each side — a wing width of about 3% of spot, or $3. Suppose the net credit is $0.90 on that $3.00 wing. Then: maximum profit $0.90 (the credit, kept if price finishes between the short strikes), maximum loss $2.10 (wing width minus credit), breakevens at roughly 7.9% below and 7.9% above spot, and the credit is 30% of the wing width. Combined short delta near zero at entry. The two ~17-delta short strikes imply roughly a 66% chance price finishes inside the band at expiration — and that is before any management.

The main risk — read this part twice

The payoff is asymmetric against you, and the direction of that asymmetry is the whole trade. In the example above you risk $2.10 to make $0.90 — you lose 2.3× what you win, and you need to be right roughly 70% of the time simply to break even before commissions. A structure that wins most months is not the same as a structure that makes money, and a single trade taken to maximum loss erases more than two winners. The specific danger is a gap: the condor's worst case is a large overnight move straight through a short strike, where there was no opportunity to adjust or close. Monday supplied examples in both directions — CrowdStrike rose 13.85% and PBF Energy fell 10.09% in one session — and that is precisely why the earnings filter exists in Section 6, and why a scheduled Fed decision deserves the same respect as an earnings date. The other, subtler risk is that a condor is short volatility: if implied volatility expands after entry, the position loses money even with price sitting perfectly still between the strikes. Position size on maximum loss, not on credit received, and decide the exit before entering, not after the gap.
